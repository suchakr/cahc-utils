# Sky, Numbers, and Time: Student Activities Connecting Ancient Indian Knowledge to the Classroom

**Audience:** Classes 8-10, Kerala schools  
**Context:** Follow-up concept note emerging from conversations during the ongoing CAHC conference, connected to the tutorial on tools for ancient Indian astronomy and cosmography

---

## Overview

These activities are designed for students with minimal equipment: mostly a stick, rope, open sky, a notebook, and a family panchanga. Each activity connects something from everyday life, such as the Sun, the Moon, birthdays, festivals, directions, or geometry, to the ways ancient Indian astronomical and mathematical traditions approached similar questions.

The activities can be run independently or as a sequence. Some require outdoor observation across multiple days. Others can be completed in one classroom or home-based session.

---

## Activity 1 - The Shanku: Finding Directions with a Stick and a Shadow

### What this is

The shanku, or gnomon, is a simple vertical stick used to study shadows. By observing its shadow carefully, students can determine the cardinal directions and understand how the Sun's motion can be used for orientation.

### What students need

- A straight stick or rod, about 30-60 cm long
- A flat, open patch of ground with sunlight
- A length of string
- Chalk, stones, or markers
- A notebook

### What students do

1. Plant the stick vertically in the ground in the morning.
2. Mark the tip of the morning shadow.
3. Draw a circle around the stick using the shadow length as radius.
4. Wait until the afternoon and mark the point where the shadow tip meets the circle again.
5. Join the two points to obtain the East-West line.
6. Draw its perpendicular to obtain the North-South line.

### What students learn

- How shadows change during the day
- How direction can be inferred from solar motion
- Why orientation mattered in older construction traditions

## Activity 2 - Watching the Sun Walk: Sunrise Horizon Observations

### What this is

If students watch sunrise from the same place over several days or weeks, they can see that the rising point is not fixed. It moves northward or southward along the horizon through the year.

### What students need

- A fixed observation point
- A clear view of the eastern horizon
- A notebook
- A distant reference point such as a tree, building, or hill

### What students do

1. Choose one observation point and keep it fixed.
2. Record where the Sun rises relative to the chosen reference.
3. Repeat the observation every few days.
4. Plot the changing sunrise points in a simple horizon sketch.

### What students learn

- The annual shift of the Sun's rising position
- The observational basis behind Uttarayana and Dakshinayana
- How patient repeated observation reveals seasonal structure

## Activity 3 - The Moon Diary: Phase, Rise Time, and Nakshatra

### What this is

Students observe the Moon over two to three weeks and record its phase, approximate rise time, and position relative to stars or nakshatra markers.

### What students need

- A notebook or observation sheet
- A clear view of the sky
- A simple nakshatra star map provided by the teacher
- A watch or phone for noting time

### What students do

1. Observe the Moon regularly over the bright fortnight or across a longer stretch if possible.
2. Record the visible phase.
3. Record when it becomes visible.
4. Note which star group or nakshatra region it is near.

### What students learn

- The visible cycle of lunar phases
- Why the Moon rises later on successive days
- The basic idea of lunar motion through nakshatra regions
- How lunar observation connects to calendrical ideas

## Activity 4 - My Family in the Panchanga

### What this is

Students use a panchanga to connect their family birthdays to tithi, nakshatra, masa, and weekday. This activity links astronomy, calendar practice, and home tradition in a very direct way.

### What students need

- A family panchanga or Malayalam almanac
- Their own date of birth and, if possible, those of a few family members
- A notebook

### What students do

1. Find their own birthday in the panchanga for the relevant year or by using a teacher-approved lookup aid.
2. Record the tithi, nakshatra, masa, and vara.
3. Repeat this for a few family members.
4. Ask at home whether birthdays are observed by Gregorian date, by nakshatra, by tithi, or by more than one method.
5. Compare when the next recurrence happens:
   Gregorian date recurrence,
   birth nakshatra recurrence,
   birth tithi recurrence.

### What students learn

- The difference between civil calendar dates and panchanga reckoning
- How lunar and solar cycles interact in lived calendar practice
- Why festival and observance dates shift in the Gregorian calendar
- How family knowledge and astronomical timekeeping connect

### Suggested student output

A small table for 3-5 family members with:

- Gregorian birth date
- Tithi
- Nakshatra
- Masa
- Family observance mode: date, star, tithi, or both
- Next recurrence by date, star, and tithi

## Activity 5 - Rajju Ganita: Geometry with a Rope

### What this is

Students use a knotted rope to construct right angles, simple geometric figures, and measured layouts. This highlights practical geometry as an embodied activity rather than only a written one.

### What students need

- A rope marked into equal units
- A flat surface
- Small stakes or markers
- A notebook

### What students do

1. Make a 3-4-5 triangle with the rope.
2. Verify that it gives a right angle.
3. Use rope-based construction to draw a circle or lay out a square.

### What students learn

- Practical geometry through measurement and construction
- The relation between observation, layout, and proof-like verification
- The continuity between astronomical orientation and geometric practice

### Note

This activity is best presented as an extension module, especially if it was not discussed in the conference sessions themselves.

## Suggested Sequencing

1. Shanku and directions
2. Sunrise observations
3. Moon diary
4. My family in the panchanga
5. Rajju Ganita

This order moves from ground and direction, to horizon observation, to lunar tracking, to calendar understanding, and finally to geometric construction.

---

## Notes for Teachers

- These activities work best when observation is emphasized before explanation.
- The panchanga activity is especially suitable in Kerala because many families already have some familiarity with star birthdays, almanacs, or related observance practices.
- The Moon and panchanga activities create a natural home-school link by involving parents and grandparents.
- Stellarium can be used as a teacher aid or optional student cross-check tool, but the core experience should remain observational and low-tech.

## Positioning Within the Conference Context

This concept note is intended as a follow-up outcome of discussions during the ongoing conference. The sessions introduced visual and computational tools for ancient Indian astronomy and cosmography for a national audience. These proposed school activities extend that conversation into the classroom by shifting from demonstration to participation: students observe, record, compare, and discuss rather than only watch.

The school track is therefore not a separate add-on, but a pedagogical continuation of the same conference themes:

- seeing the sky carefully
- relating observation to traditional categories
- connecting instruments, calendars, and geometry
- moving from curiosity to disciplined noticing
